B♭ trumpet · seven tube lengths × the overtone series

Three valves give you seven lengths of pipe. Your face picks a partial on whichever one you're holding. That's the whole instrument: a seven-by-seven table, of which nine cells are in tune and the other forty are wrong for one of exactly two reasons.

Pick a key and the chart keeps only its notes, and the scale is written out below with a fingering under every note.

The lie in the arithmetic

To drop a pitch by one semitone you have to multiply the tube by 1.0595. A valve can only add a fixed length of pipe, and the pipe it adds was cut for the open horn. Press valve 1 and the horn is already 12% longer; valve 3's tubing, still sized for the short horn, is now too short for the job it has been handed. The shortfall compounds. Nobody's tubing is cut wrong. The mistake is thinking you can add your way through a multiplication.

So the columns go progressively sharp: 1-2 by 10.6 cents, 2-3 by 15.5, 1-3 by 30.3, and all three down by 53.6. That last one is more than a quarter tone off, and no amount of care at the bench can fix it, because it isn't a manufacturing error.

The other error, and where they cancel

The rows are wrong for an unrelated reason. Partials are whole-number multiples of the fundamental and equal temperament isn't: the 3rd partial sits 1.96 cents above the tempered fifth, the 5th sits 13.7 cents below the tempered third, the 7th a full 31.2 below. Turn the two layers on and off above and you can watch which is which. Rows move together; columns move together.

Since one error lives in the rows and the other in the columns, a cell is just their sum. That is not an approximation. Cents are logarithms, the two errors are independent ratios, and they add to the last decimal place; tools/horn-check.mjs asserts a residual under 10−12 across all forty-nine cells.

Which means the flat partials are the only correction the horn owns for its sharp slots. The worst note on the instrument is written C♯4, the 3rd partial with all three valves down, where a sharp row lands on the sharpest column: +55.5 cents. And the one place the two errors nearly annihilate is written F5 taken on the 7th partial with 1-3, at −0.86 cents. The most in-tune fingering on the horn is built from the partial you're told never to use and the valve combination that is furthest wrong.

Why the slide moves

Makers already cut valve 3 long to spread the damage. Drag how long valve 3 is cut and watch it buy down 1-3 and 1-2-3 by sending slot 3 alone flat. No fixed length fixes all three, which is why the third slide has a ring on it and your left hand has a job. Kick it 18.2 mm for low D and 32.9 mm for low C♯: about three quarters of an inch, then about an inch and a third. Those two numbers come out of the tube lengths, not out of a method book, and they are what the ring is for.

What this doesn't model

  • The player. Lips bend a note by tens of cents on purpose, which is why the mic is here. Every number above is what the tube wants, not what comes out.
  • The bell and mouthpiece. Real resonances aren't exact harmonics; the flare and the cup drag them into near-alignment, and the lip mode-locks the rest. The pedal tone below partial 2 exists for that reason and is left off the chart.
  • Your actual horn. Acoustic length is taken as 1473 mm (4′10″) with ideal cuts. Bore, gap, and taper move real slides by millimeters. Set the cut slider to what your horn does and the rest follows.
  • Temperature. A cold horn is a sharp horn; nothing here knows the room.
